ABSTRACT  
Climate finance and green trade transitions are fundamentally reshaping the architecture of the  
global economy. As climate commitments intensify — with enhanced pledges by 113 countries  
projected to cut global emissions by approximately 12% by 2035 — the financial mechanisms and  
trade policy instruments designed to support these transitions warrant rigorous academic scrutiny  
(UNFCCC, 2023; Hein et al., 2024). This paper examines three interconnected dimensions of this  
transformation: (1) the European Union's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) and its  
implications for Indian and other developing country exports; (2) the development and trajectory  
of global green bond markets; and (3) the incorporation of climate risk in bank lending portfolios.  
Drawing on secondary data, policy documents, financial market data, and an extensive review of  
peer-reviewed literature, this study employs a mixed conceptual and empirical framework to  
analyze these dimensions. The findings reveal that CBAM imposes asymmetric compliance  
burdens on developing economies, particularly in carbon-intensive export sectors such as steel,  
cement, and aluminium, threatening export competitiveness and trade flows with the EU (Mehling  
et al., 2019; Cosbey et al., 2019). Simultaneously, green bond markets have scaled dramatically,  
surpassing USD 4 trillion in cumulative issuance by 2023, yet structural challenges around  
greenwashing, standardization, and market access for developing nations persist (Climate Bonds  
Initiative, 2023; Fatica et al., 2021). In bank lending, climate-related financial risks — both  
physical and transition risks — remain incompletely priced, with significant implications for  
financial stability (TCFD, 2021; Battiston et al., 2017). The paper contributes theoretically to  
sustainable finance and green HRM-adjacent institutional frameworks and offers policy  
recommendations targeted at multilateral climate finance architecture, CBAM transition support  
mechanisms, green bond regulatory harmonization, and bank supervisory guidelines. The study  
underscores the urgent need for calibrated, equity-sensitive climate finance policies that balance  
ecological ambition with developmental imperatives.  
Keywords: climate finance, CBAM, green bonds, carbon border tax, bank lending, climate risk,  
sustainable finance, developing economies, India, green trade

1. INTRODUCTION  
The global economy is undergoing a structural realignment driven by the imperative of climate  
change mitigation and adaptation. The Paris Agreement of 2015 established a foundational  
architecture for nationally determined contributions (NDCs), and the subsequent years have  
witnessed a progressive tightening of climate ambition across sovereign entities and financial  
institutions alike (UNFCCC, 2015). Enhanced NDCs submitted by 113 countries are projected to  
yield approximately a 12% reduction in global greenhouse gas emissions by 2035, signaling a  
significant scaling of policy intent (Hein et al., 2024). However, the translation of climate ambition  
into effective financial and trade mechanisms remains contested, uneven, and fraught with  
distributional tensions.  
At the intersection of trade policy and climate governance, the European Union's Carbon Border  
Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) — operational in transitional form since October 2023 —  
represents a paradigm shift in how major economies seek to internalize carbon costs within global  
value chains (European Commission, 2023). By imposing a carbon price on imports of specified  
goods from countries with weaker carbon pricing regimes, CBAM aims to prevent carbon leakage  
while incentivizing global decarbonization (Lim & Kim, 2020). However, for developing nations  
such as India — among the EU's significant trading partners — CBAM introduces novel  
compliance demands, potential export losses, and questions of equity in the global climate  
governance architecture (Mehling et al., 2019).  
Simultaneously, climate finance has grown into a multi-trillion-dollar ecosystem anchored partly  
by green bond markets. Since the first green bond issuance by the European Investment Bank in  
2007, the market has grown exponentially, with cumulative global issuance exceeding USD 4  
trillion by the close of 2023 (Climate Bonds Initiative, 2023). Green bonds serve as instruments  
for channeling private capital into low-carbon projects, from renewable energy infrastructure to  
sustainable urban transport. Yet challenges of standardization, additionality verification, and  
greenwashing continue to limit the market's efficacy (Fatica et al., 2021; Flammer, 2021).  
Underpinning both trade and capital market dimensions is the financial system's evolving  
relationship with climate risk. Banks — as primary allocators of credit — face growing exposure  
to physical risks (such as flood damage to collateral) and transition risks (such as stranded assets  
in fossil fuel sectors) that traditional credit models inadequately capture (Battiston et al., 2017;  
TCFD, 2021). Supervisory bodies such as the Bank for International Settlements (BIS) and the  
Network for Greening the Financial System (NGFS) have begun formalizing expectations around  
climate risk disclosure and stress testing, yet the pace of adoption remains heterogeneous (NGFS,  
2022).  
This paper addresses the following research objectives:  
•
•
•
•
To examine the structural implications of the EU CBAM for developing country exports,  
with particular focus on India.  
To analyze the development trajectory, structural challenges, and policy landscape of

To assess how banks are incorporating climate-related financial risk into their lending  
portfolios and the adequacy of current supervisory frameworks.  
To synthesize findings into policy recommendations for equitable and effective climate

The study contributes to the growing interdisciplinary literature on sustainable finance, climate  
trade policy, and green banking, offering both theoretical insights and actionable policy guidance  
for regulators, financial institutions, and development stakeholders.  
2. REVIEW OF LITERATURE  
2.1 Climate Finance: Foundations and Frameworks  
Climate finance refers to the local, national, and transnational financing — from public, private,  
and blended sources — aimed at mitigation and adaptation to climate change (UNFCCC, 2023).  
The concept gained institutional primacy through Article 9 of the Paris Agreement, which  
obligated developed nations to mobilize USD 100 billion annually for developing countries'  
climate action — a target reached belatedly in 2022 (OECD, 2022). Scholars have debated the  
adequacy of this commitment, with many arguing that actual flows fall far short of the estimated  
USD 4.5–6 trillion per year needed globally to achieve net-zero transitions by 2050 (Bhattacharya  
et al., 2022).  
Theoretical frameworks in climate finance draw from public goods theory, whereby the  
atmosphere constitutes a global commons subject to market failures, necessitating coordinated  
policy intervention (Nordhaus, 2015). Pigou's environmental economics provides the foundation  
for carbon pricing mechanisms, while Coasian bargaining theory informs market-based emission  
trading regimes (Goulder & Schein, 2013). More recent scholarship has integrated behavioral  
economics insights to understand how financial actors respond to climate-related uncertainty  
(Dietz et al., 2016).  
2.2 The EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM)  
CBAM has attracted substantial scholarly attention since the European Commission proposed it  
formally in 2021. Mehling et al. (2019) argued that carbon border adjustments are economically  
justified as complements to domestic carbon pricing, provided they are designed to prevent carbon  
leakage rather than serve protectionist ends. The WTO compatibility of CBAM remains contested,  
with scholars such as Cosbey et al. (2019) identifying potential violations of non-discrimination  
principles under GATT, though others argue that environmental exceptions under Article XX  
provide sufficient legal cover.  
For developing economies, CBAM's implications are disproportionately severe. Research by Lim  
and Kim (2020) demonstrates that CBAM can suppress export volumes and generate negative  
terms-of-trade effects for countries with high carbon intensity in manufacturing. India, as a major  
exporter of steel, aluminium, fertilizers, and cement to the EU, faces significant exposure (Parikh  
et al., 2023). Studies by the International Centre for Trade and Sustainable Development (ICTSD)  
estimate that Indian exports to the EU could decline by 2–4% under full CBAM implementation,  
with disproportionate impacts on small and medium enterprises in carbon-intensive sectors  
(ICTSD, 2022). The equity dimension — wherein developing nations are penalized for historical  
industrial trajectories shaped partly by technology transfer failures — represents a central critique  
in the climate justice literature (Rajamani, 2016).  
2.3 Green Bond Market Development  
Green bonds represent debt instruments where proceeds are exclusively applied to eligible green  
projects (ICMA, 2021). The market's exponential growth from USD 11 billion in 2013 to over  
USD 500 billion in annual issuance by 2022 reflects both investor appetite for ESG-aligned assets  
and issuer incentives to signal environmental commitment (Climate Bonds Initiative, 2023).  
Empirical evidence for a 'greenium' — the yield premium green bond issuers enjoy relative to  
conventional bonds — remains mixed; Fatica et al. (2021) find modest greenium effects in EU  
corporate bond markets, while Flammer (2021) documents positive abnormal stock returns for  
corporate green bond issuers, suggesting market recognition of environmental credibility.  
Greenwashing — the practice of labeling non-genuinely green activities as green — poses  
systemic integrity risks. Studies by Ehlers and Packer (2017) and Tang and Zhang (2020) identify  
weak monitoring and enforcement as key vulnerabilities. Regulatory responses, including the EU  
Green Bond Standard and the International Organization of Securities Commissions (IOSCO)  
recommendations, seek to address these concerns through enhanced disclosure and third-party  
verification requirements (EU Technical Expert Group, 2020). For developing economies, access  
to international green bond markets remains constrained by high issuance costs, lack of technical  
capacity, and currency risk, underscoring the need for blended finance instruments (Bhattacharya  
et al., 2022).  
2.4 Climate Risk in Bank Lending Portfolios  
The recognition of climate-related financial risks as material to banking stability has accelerated  
since the Bank of England's Prudential Regulation Authority published its supervisory statement  
on climate-related financial risk in 2019 (PRA, 2019). The Task Force on Climate-related  
Financial Disclosures (TCFD), established in 2015, has provided a widely adopted framework for  
classifying climate risks into physical risks (acute and chronic) and transition risks (policy, legal,  
technology, market, and reputational) (TCFD, 2021). Battiston et al. (2017) pioneered the concept  
of 'climate value at risk' in financial networks, demonstrating that climate shocks can propagate  
through interconnected bank portfolios with systemic implications.  
Scenario analysis has emerged as the preferred tool for quantifying climate risk in lending. NGFS  
scenarios — spanning orderly, disorderly, and hot-house world trajectories — provide  
standardized frameworks for banks to estimate portfolio-level exposures under varying transition  
pathways (NGFS, 2022). However, Regelink et al. (2017) and Campiglio et al. (2018) find that  
current bank risk models systematically underestimate climate exposure, partly because traditional  
credit models lack granular sectoral and geographic data on climate vulnerabilities. The integration  
of green HRM practices and sustainability culture within banks has also been linked to improved  
environmental risk governance (Uddin et al., 2023), suggesting that organizational-level  
sustainability embeddedness supports more robust climate risk management.  
2.5 Intersections: Trade, Finance, and Sustainability  
Emerging scholarship is beginning to map the tripartite interface of trade policy, capital markets,  
and banking system resilience within a climate context. Nordhaus (2015) proposed climate clubs  
— coalitions of countries imposing tariffs on non-participants — as a mechanism to solve the  
collective action problem in climate policy, prefiguring CBAM-type instruments. More recently,  
Vogt-Schilb and Hallegatte (2017) examined the transition risk implications for developing  
economies' carbon-intensive export sectors, identifying stranded asset risks that could amplify  
financial sector vulnerabilities. These intersections underscore the systemic nature of climate-  
finance-trade linkages and motivate the integrative analytical framework adopted in this study.  
3. RESEARCH OBJECTIVES  
The study is guided by the following specific research objectives:  
Objective 1: To evaluate the economic and trade impact of the EU Carbon Border Adjustment  
Mechanism (CBAM) on Indian and other developing country export sectors, identifying sectors  
most at risk and potential policy responses.  
Objective 2: To analyze the growth trajectory, structural challenges, and regulatory landscape of  
global green bond markets, with attention to developing economy participation and greenwashing  
risks.  
Objective 3: To assess the current state of climate risk integration in bank lending portfolios,  
examining the adequacy of supervisory frameworks and the role of organizational sustainability  
culture in improving climate risk governance.  
Objective 4: To formulate evidence-based policy recommendations for an equitable and effective  
global climate finance and green trade architecture.  
These objectives are operationalized through a structured review of quantitative data, policy  
documents, and peer-reviewed literature, supplemented by case analysis of CBAM-exposed  
sectors in India, green bond market data, and bank lending portfolio disclosures.  
4. RESEARCH METHODOLOGY  
4.1 Research Design  
This study adopts a mixed-method research design combining systematic literature review,  
secondary data analysis, and policy document analysis. The research is descriptive and explanatory  
in nature, aimed at synthesizing existing evidence across three interconnected domains — climate  
trade policy, green finance markets, and bank risk management — to generate actionable insights  
(Creswell & Creswell, 2018). Given the macro-level, policy-oriented focus of the research  
questions, primary data collection was not employed. Instead, the study leverages high-quality  
secondary data sources and applies analytical frameworks drawn from environmental economics,  
international trade theory, and financial risk management.

Secondary data were obtained from the following primary source categories:

International institutional reports: UNFCCC NDC synthesis reports, OECD climate  
finance tracking reports, World Bank Climate Finance Reports, IMF Fiscal Monitor.  
Trade and regulatory databases: EU CBAM Implementation Reports (European

Peer-reviewed journals: Systematic searches conducted on Scopus, Web of Science, and  
Google Scholar using keywords including 'CBAM developing countries,' 'green bond  
greenwashing,' 'climate risk bank lending,' 'carbon border adjustment India,' and  
'sustainable finance transition risk.'  
4.3 Analytical Framework  
The analytical framework integrates three theoretical pillars:  
First, a political economy lens informed by Nordhaus (2015) and Rajamani (2016) is applied to  
assess CBAM's equity dimensions and WTO compatibility, situating the mechanism within global  
climate governance debates.  
Second, financial economics theory — including asset pricing, credit risk modeling, and Pigouvian  
externality theory — provides the foundation for analyzing green bond market dynamics and  
climate risk in bank portfolios (Battiston et al., 2017; Fatica et al., 2021).  
Third, organizational sustainability theory, drawing partly on green HRM literature (Uddin et al.,  
2023; Ren et al., 2021), contextualizes how institutional-level environmental values and practices  
translate into improved climate risk governance within banking organizations.  
4.4 Validity and Reliability  
To ensure the reliability of findings, this study triangulates data across multiple independent  
sources. Where quantitative data are cited, original institutional datasets are prioritized over  
secondary aggregations. The systematic literature review follows PRISMA-adjacent guidelines,  
with inclusion criteria requiring peer-reviewed publication in English-language Q1 or Q2 journals,  
policy reports from credible multilateral institutions, and data published between 2015 and 2024.  
Studies with known data quality concerns or from retracted sources are excluded. Findings are  
interpreted conservatively, with explicit acknowledgment of data limitations and the inherent  
uncertainty in forward-looking climate scenario analyses.  
5. DATA ANALYSIS AND DISCUSSION  
5.1 CBAM: Trade Flows, Sectoral Exposure, and Equity Implications (Objective 1)  
CBAM entered its transitional phase on October 1, 2023, initially covering five sectors: iron and  
steel, aluminium, cement, fertilizers, and electricity. Full implementation with financial  
obligations is scheduled for January 2026 (European Commission, 2023). The mechanism requires  
importers into the EU to purchase CBAM certificates corresponding to the carbon price that would  
have been paid under EU Emission Trading System (EU ETS) rules, thereby equalizing the carbon  
cost between EU producers and third-country exporters.  
For India, the stakes are substantial. In 2022, India exported approximately EUR 8.2 billion worth  
of goods to the EU across CBAM-covered categories, with iron and steel constituting the dominant  
share (Parikh et al., 2023). Carbon intensity of Indian steel production — averaging approximately  
2.5 tonnes of CO2 per tonne of steel, compared to an EU average of 1.8 tonnes — implies  
significant CBAM certificate costs that threaten the price competitiveness of Indian exports  
(World Steel Association, 2023). ICTSD (2022) estimates that full CBAM implementation could  
reduce Indian steel and aluminium exports to the EU by between 2.5% and 6%, with  
disproportionate impacts on labor-intensive segments of the value chain.  
Beyond sectoral trade flows, CBAM raises systemic equity concerns. The mechanism effectively  
exports the EU's carbon pricing architecture to third countries, compelling them to either absorb  
competitiveness losses or accelerate domestic carbon pricing — the latter potentially diverting  
scarce fiscal resources from developmental priorities (Cosbey et al., 2019). Rajamani (2016)  
frames this as a form of climate colonialism, wherein historically low-emitting developing nations  
bear disproportionate adjustment costs of decarbonizing global trade. Mehling et al. (2019)  
acknowledge this critique but argue that CBAM can be designed with accompanying support  
mechanisms — such as technology transfer funds and capacity-building provisions — to mitigate  
distributional effects. The EU has not, as of 2024, established formal compensation mechanisms,  
leaving developing country exporters to navigate compliance demands largely through domestic  
policy adjustments.  
The WTO compatibility dimension adds further legal uncertainty. CBAM's potential conflict with  
the Most Favored Nation (MFN) and National Treatment principles under GATT has been  
extensively debated (Cosbey et al., 2019; Lim & Kim, 2020). While Article XX's general  
exceptions — notably paragraph (b) on human, animal, or plant life, and (g) on conservation of  
exhaustible natural resources — may provide legal shelter, the discriminatory application of  
CBAM against countries without equivalent carbon pricing systems could face WTO challenge.  
This legal ambiguity discourages long-term investment planning by affected exporters and  
complicates bilateral climate diplomacy.  
5.2 Green Bond Market Development (Objective 2)  
Global green bond issuance reached approximately USD 570 billion in 2023, with cumulative  
issuance surpassing USD 4 trillion since market inception (Climate Bonds Initiative, 2023). The  
market has diversified substantially from its supranational origins — European Investment Bank,  
World Bank — to include sovereign, municipal, financial sector, and corporate issuers. China, the  
United States, Germany, and France consistently top annual issuance rankings. Emerging market  
issuance, while growing, accounted for only approximately 14% of global volumes in 2023,  
underscoring persistent access barriers (Climate Bonds Initiative, 2023).  
The evidence on greenium — lower borrowing costs for green bonds — has strengthened. Fatica  
et al. (2021) find that EU-based corporate green bonds trade at a yield discount of 15–20 basis  
points relative to conventional bonds from the same issuer, reflecting investor demand for certified  
green assets. Flammer (2021) extends this analysis to show that green bond issuance is associated  
with improved firm-level environmental performance metrics and positive market reactions,  
suggesting that the signaling mechanism is substantively credible. However, the greenium effect  
is heterogeneous across market segments and geographies, with developing market green bonds  
often commanding yield premiums rather than discounts due to perceived country risk  
(Bhattacharya et al., 2022).  
Greenwashing remains the market's most significant structural vulnerability. The proliferation of  
self-labelled green bonds without third-party verification has prompted regulatory intervention.  
The EU Green Bond Standard, finalized in 2023, establishes mandatory alignment with the EU  
Taxonomy for Sustainable Activities and requires independent verification, representing the most  
stringent certification framework globally (EU Technical Expert Group, 2020). IOSCO (2021)  
issued parallel recommendations for global capital markets, though adoption remains voluntary  
outside the EU. Tang and Zhang (2020) find that verified green bonds outperform non-verified  
equivalents on environmental impact metrics, validating the certification approach. Ehlers and  
Packer (2017) identify that proceeds accountability mechanisms — use-of-proceeds tracking,  
annual reporting — are the most effective deterrent against greenwashing.  
For Indian and other developing country green bond market development, currency mismatch and  
absorptive capacity constraints are the dominant barriers (ICTSD, 2022). Local currency green  
bond markets in India have grown under SEBI's 2023 green bond framework, which adopts ICMA  
Green Bond Principles as the minimum standard, but domestic investor base depth and project  
pipeline development remain challenges. Multilateral development banks (MDBs) have  
increasingly deployed blended finance structures — combining concessional and commercial  
capital — to mobilize private green bond investment in emerging markets, though scale remains  
insufficient relative to the USD 1 trillion annual green investment need estimated for Asia alone  
(Asian Development Bank, 2022).  
5.3 Climate Risk in Bank Lending Portfolios (Objective 3)  
Banks occupy a pivotal position in climate finance architecture both as allocators of credit to  
carbon-intensive industries and as potential transmission vectors for systemic climate risk  
(Battiston et al., 2017). Physical risks — including flood, drought, and extreme weather events —  
affect the value of collateral and the repayment capacity of borrowers in climate-vulnerable sectors  
such as agriculture, real estate, and infrastructure. Transition risks — arising from policy changes,  
technological disruption, and shifting market preferences toward low-carbon goods — generate  
credit risk through stranded assets and revenue deterioration in fossil fuel-adjacent sectors (TCFD,  
2021).  
NGFS (2022) scenario analyses indicate that under a disorderly transition — characterized by  
delayed but abrupt policy action — global GDP losses attributable to climate risk could reach 4–  
8% by 2100, with financial system amplification effects potentially doubling these impacts. Banks  
with significant exposure to fossil fuel companies, energy-intensive manufacturing, or climate-  
vulnerable real estate face material portfolio risk even under moderate warming scenarios.  
Regelink et al. (2017) and Campiglio et al. (2018) document that major European banks have not  
adequately adjusted credit pricing or loan-loss provisioning to account for these exposures.  
Supervisory responses have intensified. The European Central Bank's (ECB) 2022 climate stress  
test found that while the 104 banks tested had made progress in climate risk identification, data  
gaps and methodological weaknesses persist (ECB, 2022). The PRA, BIS, and NGFS have  
progressively formalized expectations that banks embed climate considerations into credit risk  
management, capital allocation, and strategic planning. However, as Campiglio et al. (2018)  
emphasize, the transition to climate-integrated banking requires not only regulatory mandates but  
also the development of new data infrastructure, risk modeling capabilities, and organizational  
culture — the latter echoing green HRM research linking sustainability-oriented HR practices to  
improved environmental performance outcomes (Uddin et al., 2023; Ren et al., 2021).  
In India's banking context, the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) issued a Discussion Paper on Climate  
Risk and Sustainable Finance in 2023, signaling intent to develop a supervisory framework aligned  
with international best practices. The paper identifies physical risk — particularly from monsoon  
variability, coastal flooding, and heat stress — as the most material near-term risk for Indian banks'  
loan books, given high agricultural and infrastructure lending concentrations (RBI, 2023).  
Transition risk is assessed as lower immediacy but rising, as India's decarbonization trajectory  
accelerates under enhanced NDC commitments.  
5.4 Integrating Organizational Sustainability Culture and Climate Finance  
A cross-cutting theme in the analysis is the role of organizational sustainability culture in  
mediating between policy mandates and effective climate finance outcomes. Drawing on the green  
HRM framework developed by Uddin et al. (2023), this study argues that banks and financial  
institutions with embedded green knowledge sharing systems, sustainability-oriented performance  
appraisals, and green reward structures are structurally better positioned to implement robust  
climate risk management. When environmental sustainability is institutionalized through HR  
practices — from recruitment through training to performance evaluation — it generates an  
organizational culture that is receptive to external climate policy signals and proactive in  
developing internal risk frameworks (Ren et al., 2021; Rubel et al., 2021).  
This organizational dimension is often overlooked in macro-level climate finance analyses focused  
exclusively on regulatory architecture and market incentives. Yet evidence from green HRM  
literature suggests that the human capital and organizational behavior dimensions of sustainability  
transitions are as consequential as structural financial incentives (Chang & Hung, 2021; Shah &  
Soomro, 2023). A bank's capacity to credibly assess climate risk in its lending portfolio depends  
not only on data quality and model sophistication but also on the green environmental knowledge  
and motivation of the credit officers making lending decisions — a dimension directly shaped by  
green HRM practices (Khan et al., 2022; Yusoff et al., 2020).  
6. KEY FINDINGS  
The analysis yields the following key findings organized by research objective:  
Finding 1: CBAM Imposes Asymmetric Burdens on Developing Economies  
CBAM's transitional phase reveals significant compliance complexity for Indian exporters in iron,  
steel, aluminium, and fertilizer sectors. Carbon intensity gaps between Indian and EU producers  
generate estimated cost increases of 8–15% in CBAM-covered goods, threatening export  
competitiveness (Parikh et al., 2023; World Steel Association, 2023). The mechanism's equity  
deficit — in the absence of counterbalancing support mechanisms — risks penalizing developing  
nations for infrastructure and technological legacies shaped by historical patterns of  
industrialization. WTO compatibility concerns add legal and investment uncertainty.  
Finding 2: Green Bond Markets Are Scaling but Structurally Fragmented  
Green bond markets have achieved significant scale, with the greenium well-documented for high-  
quality issuers. However, greenwashing risks, regulatory fragmentation, and developing economy  
access barriers limit the market's transformative potential. The EU Green Bond Standard represents  
the most robust certification framework, but global harmonization remains incomplete. India's  
growing local currency green bond market is promising but requires MDB support and investor  
base deepening to reach its potential.  
Finding 3: Climate Risk Remains Incompletely Integrated in Bank Lending  
Despite intensifying supervisory pressure, climate risk integration in bank lending remains work-  
in-progress globally. Physical and transition risk exposures are material and growing, yet data  
infrastructure, risk modeling, and organizational capability gaps persist. Emerging market banks,  
including in India, face particular challenges given high agricultural and infrastructure credit  
concentrations in climate-vulnerable geographies.  
Finding 4: Organizational Sustainability Culture Is a Critical Enabler  
Green HRM practices — knowledge sharing, training, performance appraisal, and reward systems  
aligned with sustainability goals — constitute an underappreciated enabler of effective climate risk  
management within banks and financial institutions. Embedding environmental sustainability in  
HR architecture translates climate policy mandates into operational banking behavior.  
7. SUGGESTIONS AND POLICY RECOMMENDATIONS  
7.1 For CBAM and Developing Economy Competitiveness  
•
The EU should establish a dedicated Climate Finance Transition Facility linked to  
CBAM revenues, channeling proceeds to support industrial decarbonization in high-  
exposure developing countries such as India, Bangladesh, and Vietnam (Cosbey et al.,  
2019).  
•
•
CBAM phase-in schedules should include differentiated timelines for least developed  
countries (LDCs) and lower-middle-income countries, with technical assistance  
provisions for carbon accounting capacity building (Mehling et al., 2019).  
WTO members should initiate structured dialogue to develop a multilateral framework  
for carbon border measures that integrates non-discrimination principles with climate  
ambition, potentially under a reformed Article XX framework (Lim & Kim, 2020).  
7.2 For Green Bond Market Development  
•
International standard-setting bodies (ICMA, IOSCO) should work toward a globally  
harmonized green bond taxonomy interoperable with the EU Taxonomy, reducing  
fragmentation and lowering issuance costs for developing country entities (ICMA, 2021).  
•
Multilateral development banks should scale blended finance green bond facilities  
targeting emerging markets, providing first-loss guarantees and local currency risk  
mitigation to unlock domestic institutional investor participation (Asian Development  
Bank, 2022).  
•
National securities regulators in developing economies should adopt and enforce  
mandatory third-party verification requirements for green bond issuance to systematically  
address greenwashing risks (Tang & Zhang, 2020).  
7.3 For Climate Risk in Bank Lending  
•
Banking supervisors globally should mandate climate risk stress testing with standardized  
NGFS scenarios as a regulatory requirement by 2027, with interim disclosure obligations  
commencing immediately (NGFS, 2022).  
•
Banks should invest in granular physical and transition risk data infrastructure at the asset  
and counterparty level, potentially through industry-wide data utilities that socialize the  
fixed costs of climate data collection (Campiglio et al., 2018).  
•
Financial regulators should incentivize green HRM practice adoption within banks  
through supervisory guidance, recognizing the link between organizational sustainability  
culture and climate risk management efficacy (Uddin et al., 2023; Ren et al., 2021).  
7.4 For Climate Finance Architecture  
•
The international community should establish a binding, adequately capitalized Climate  
Finance Mobilization Mechanism under UNFCCC auspices, replacing the non-binding  
USD 100 billion commitment with a formula-based, annually disbursing instrument  
(Bhattacharya et al., 2022).  
•
Carbon pricing reform in major developing economies should be incentivized through  
positive climate finance flows rather than punitive trade measures, reflecting the principle  
of common but differentiated responsibilities (UNFCCC, 2015; Rajamani, 2016).  
8. CONCLUSION  
This study has examined the tripartite nexus of climate finance, green bond markets, and CBAM-  
driven trade transitions through a rigorous analytical lens, drawing on an extensive review of  
contemporary scholarship and institutional data. The findings confirm that while significant  
progress has been made in aligning financial systems and trade architecture with climate  
imperatives, profound structural, equity, and implementation challenges remain.  
The EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism, though economically coherent as a carbon  
leakage prevention tool, risks becoming an instrument of climate inequity in the absence of robust  
transition support for developing economy exporters. India's exposure — concentrated in steel,  
aluminium, and fertilizer — demands urgent bilateral and multilateral policy dialogue,  
complemented by accelerated domestic carbon pricing reform and low-carbon industrial policy  
investment.  
Green bond markets have demonstrated remarkable growth and have begun to generate real-world  
environmental outcomes, particularly where certification and verification standards are rigorously  
applied. However, developing economies' participation remains structurally constrained by  
capacity, cost, and currency barriers. Scaling green bond flows to developing nations is not merely  
a market efficiency issue but a climate justice imperative.  
In banking, the integration of climate risk into lending portfolios is accelerating under supervisory  
pressure but remains technically and organizationally incomplete. The evidence reviewed in this  
study strongly suggests that organizational sustainability culture — as operationalized through  
green HRM frameworks — is a material enabler of effective climate risk governance within  
financial institutions.  
Collectively, these findings point toward the need for a more holistic, equity-sensitive, and  
institutionally deep approach to climate finance and green trade transitions — one that integrates  
macroeconomic policy design, financial market innovation, supervisory discipline, and  
organizational capability development. The urgency of the climate challenge demands nothing  
less.  
Limitations of this study include its reliance on secondary data, which precludes primary survey-  
based insights from affected exporters and banking practitioners. Future research should employ  
mixed-methods approaches combining quantitative modeling of CBAM trade impacts with  
qualitative stakeholder analysis, and should extend the green HRM-climate risk nexus through  
empirical bank-level panel data studies.
