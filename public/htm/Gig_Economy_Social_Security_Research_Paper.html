Bridging the Protection Gap: A Systematic Analysis of Social Security  
Deficits in the Gig Economy and Organisational–Governmental Pathways  
Toward Inclusive Platform Work

ABSTRACT  
The rapid globalisation of the platform economy has produced a structurally significant yet  
chronically under-protected labour segment: the gig worker. Worldwide, an estimated 154 to  
435 million individuals now participate in platform-mediated work, constituting between 4.4%  
and 12.5% of the global labour force (World Bank, 2024). Despite this scale, the vast majority  
of these workers remain excluded from fundamental social protections — including  
unemployment insurance, health coverage, pension entitlements, and occupational safety  
provisions — because dominant legal frameworks continue to classify them as independent  
contractors rather than employees. This paper conducts a systematic analytical review of the  
social security gaps confronting gig and platform workers, with a dual focus: first, diagnosing  
the structural mechanisms that perpetuate these gaps; and second, identifying evidence-based  
pathways through which organisations and governments can extend meaningful employment  
protections and social security benefits to this workforce. Drawing on secondary data from  
legislative developments across India, the European Union, the United Kingdom, the United  
States, and Brazil, and guided theoretically by Segmented Labour Market Theory and the  
Capability Approach, this study identifies four thematic dimensions of the protection gap: legal  
classification deficits, income and welfare insecurity, algorithmic management and power  
asymmetry, and regulatory fragmentation. The study further develops an Integrated Protection  
Framework synthesising portable benefits models, tripartite funding mechanisms, algorithmic  
transparency mandates, and sector-specific welfare boards as actionable policy prescriptions.  
The findings carry direct implications for human resource management scholars, labour  
economists, platform companies, and policymakers navigating the governance of the emerging  
digital labour market.  
Keywords: gig economy, platform workers, social security, employment protection, labour  
law, portable benefits, algorithmic management, India, EU Platform Work Directive

1. Introduction  
The nature of work has undergone a fundamental transformation in the twenty-first century,  
driven in large part by the proliferation of digital labour platforms. From ride-hailing and food  
delivery to professional freelancing and on-demand domestic services, the gig economy has  
expanded into virtually every sector of the global economy. As of 2025, approximately 435  
million people worldwide engage in some form of gig work, including freelance, contract, and  
platform-based employment, representing between 4.4% and 12.5% of the global labour force  
(Jain, 2024). Over 1.1 billion jobs were completed through gig platforms in 2023 alone,  
underscoring the economic scale and structural importance of this mode of employment.  
Despite this expansion, the structural benefits that traditional employment relationships afford  
— minimum wage protections, unemployment insurance, health coverage, pension  
contributions, collective bargaining rights, and occupational safety regulation — remain  
largely inaccessible to gig workers. Platform companies systematically classify workers as  
independent contractors, a legal designation that, while offering workers nominal autonomy  
and flexibility, simultaneously absolves platforms of the financial and regulatory burdens  
associated with employer status (Human Rights Watch, 2025). The consequence is a structural  
protection gap that exposes millions of workers to income volatility, health insecurity,  
retirement vulnerability, and workplace exploitation without legal recourse.  
The severity of this gap is illustrated by recent data from the European Union, where Eurostat  
records show that 62.4% of platform workers lack unemployment insurance and 56.3% lack  
sickness coverage (Premier Science, 2025). In the United States, Human Rights Watch (2025)  
estimates that Texas alone could have collected over USD 111 million in unemployment  
insurance contributions between 2020 and 2022 if platform workers had been classified as  
employees. In India, despite the landmark Code on Social Security (2020) providing formal  
legal recognition to gig and platform workers for the first time, the Code's operational  
provisions remain at a nascent stage of implementation (Bhatt and Joshi Associates, 2025).  
The academic literature has increasingly engaged with the gig economy, examining dimensions  
such as algorithmic management, worker autonomy, income precarity, and platform  
governance (Kadolkar et al., 2025; Alauddin et al., 2025; Wood et al., 2019). However, a  
critical gap remains: the literature has not yet produced a comprehensive, multi-jurisdictional  
analytical framework that simultaneously diagnoses the structural mechanisms perpetuating  
social security deficits and prescribes actionable, evidence-based pathways for their resolution  
by both organisations and governments. This paper addresses that gap directly.  
The present study is guided by the following research questions: (1) What are the key structural  
mechanisms through which the gig economy perpetuates social security gaps for platform  
workers? (2) How are organisations and governments internationally responding to these gaps,  
and with what effectiveness? (3) What integrated policy and organisational framework can  
bridge the protection deficit in the platform economy? This study contributes to the literature  
in three ways: first, by providing a theoretically grounded diagnostic of the four-dimensional  
protection gap; second, by conducting a comparative cross-jurisdictional analysis of legislative  
and organisational responses; and third, by proposing an Integrated Protection Framework that  
synthesises actionable prescriptions for both government regulators and platform  
organisations.  
2. Theoretical Framework  
This study is anchored in two complementary theoretical frameworks: Segmented Labour  
Market Theory and Sen's Capability Approach. The selection of these frameworks is purposive,  
each offering a distinct but mutually reinforcing analytical lens for understanding the structural  
determinants and human development consequences of the gig economy's social security  
deficit.  
2.1 Segmented Labour Market Theory  
Segmented Labour Market Theory (SLMT), originally developed by Doeringer and Piore  
(1971) and later extended by Piore (1975), posits that labour markets are not unified and  
competitive but are structurally divided into primary and secondary segments. Primary labour  
market jobs are characterised by stable employment, good wages, benefits, promotion  
opportunities, and institutional protections. Secondary market jobs, by contrast, are  
characterised by low wages, instability, limited benefits, and high turnover. Gig and platform  
work constitutes a paradigmatic example of secondary labour market employment, amplified  
and globalised through digital intermediation.  
The contemporary platform economy has reproduced and intensified classic secondary labour  
market characteristics. Platform companies leverage the contractor designation to confine  
workers to a secondary market status — algorithmically managed, economically dependent,  
institutionally invisible — while retaining the control mechanisms that characterise employer–  
employee relationships. Johnston and Land-Kazlauskas (2019), as cited in Alauddin et al.  
(2025), observe that gig workers typically have limited bargaining power, with platforms  
controlling the terms and conditions of work, including pricing, ratings, and task allocation.  
SLMT provides a structural explanation for why market forces alone cannot correct the  
protection gap: the segmentation is institutionally reproduced through classification law,  
platform design, and regulatory inertia.  
2.2 The Capability Approach  
Sen's Capability Approach (1999) offers the normative evaluative lens for this study. The  
Capability Approach holds that development and social justice should be evaluated not merely  
by income or GDP, but by what individuals are substantively free to be and to do — their real  
opportunities to lead lives they have reason to value. Social security, in this framework, is not  
merely a welfare transfer mechanism but a foundational enabler of human capabilities: health  
security enables the capability for physical integrity; income protection enables the capability  
for economic agency; occupational safety enables the capability for bodily safety; and access  
to dispute resolution enables the capability for political voice.  
Applied to the gig economy, the Capability Approach reveals that the absence of social  
protection does not merely reduce workers' incomes; it structurally constrains their capability  
sets, limiting their ability to take occupational risks, invest in skill development, access  
healthcare, or exercise labour rights. This framework aligns closely with the analytical  
approach adopted in the reference paper by Uddin et al. (2023), which applied the green HRM  
literature's capability-enhancing logic to demonstrate how institutional interventions can  
expand workers' functional capabilities. The same logic is applied here: extending social  
security to gig workers is not a charitable act but a structural requirement for achieving outcome  
equity in the platform economy.  
3. Review of Literature  
3.1 The Structural Origins of the Protection Gap  
The protection deficit confronting gig workers originates in a fundamental legal architecture  
mismatch. Contemporary social security systems in most jurisdictions were designed in the  
twentieth century to provide protections to standard employees in long-term, bilateral  
employment relationships. The gig economy has disrupted this architecture by introducing a  
tripartite or multi-party labour relationship involving the worker, the platform, and the end-  
customer, with no clearly defined employer (Schor, 2020). This structural ambiguity is  
deliberately exploited by platforms to avoid employer-level obligations.  
Human Rights Watch (2025) documents this exploitation comprehensively in a 155-page  
investigative report on seven major US platforms — including Amazon Flex, DoorDash, Lyft,  
Uber, and Instacart — finding systematic algorithmic wage manipulation, misclassification,  
and regulatory arbitrage. The report notes that Uber, with 76% of the US rideshare market,  
generated USD 43.9 billion in revenue in 2024 and a net income of USD 9.8 billion, yet its  
workers lack access to minimum wage protections, overtime pay, unemployment insurance, or  
workers' compensation. DoorDash, controlling 67% of the US food delivery market, generated  
USD 10.72 billion in revenue in 2024 while its workers often earn as little as USD 2 per hour  
in low-demand periods.  
This revenue–protection asymmetry is a defining structural feature of the platform economy.  
As Schor (2020) observes, while algorithms are effective in completing task allocation, they  
systematically sustain inequalities and power relations within society. Workers experience a  
paradox of nominal freedom combined with structural coercion: they have formal autonomy  
over their working hours but are algorithmically incentivised through penalty systems, surge  
pricing, and acceptance rate monitoring to work in ways that serve platform profitability at the  
cost of personal welfare (Kadolkar et al., 2025).  
3.2 Dimensions of Social Security Deficits  
The literature identifies several interconnected dimensions of the gig economy's social security  
deficit. First, the absence of unemployment insurance exposes workers to acute income shocks.  
The COVID-19 pandemic revealed this vulnerability dramatically: gig workers were  
disproportionately affected by the economic downturn because their contractor status excluded  
them from emergency wage support schemes in most jurisdictions (Alauddin et al., 2025).  
Second, health and occupational safety gaps are significant and well-documented. In India, gig  
workers were classified as part of the "missing middle" — individuals who are too  
economically active to qualify for government-funded health schemes but too financially  
constrained to afford private insurance (HDFC Bank, 2025). The 2025–26 Union Budget's  
extension of healthcare coverage to approximately 10 million gig workers under the Ayushman  
Bharat Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (AB-PMJAY) represents a policy milestone, yet  
critics note that successful implementation requires data-sharing compliance from aggregators  
and portal integration that remain operationally untested (HDFC Bank, 2025).  
Third, old-age income insecurity is a growing concern. Online gig workers, even those with  
relatively higher earnings, show consistently low social insurance coverage. A World Bank  
survey in Pakistan's Khyber Pakhtunkhwa province found that only 41% of online gig workers  
earn less than USD 200 monthly, yet their pension and retirement savings participation remains  
minimal (World Bank, 2024). The structural reason is that contribution-based pension systems  
require regular, predictable income — a condition that gig income volatility systematically  
undermines.  
Fourth, algorithmic management creates psychosocial risks that current occupational safety  
frameworks do not adequately address. Research by Kadolkar et al. (2025) demonstrates that  
algorithmic penalty systems increase precarity among food delivery couriers, generating stress  
and pressure to accept low-paying assignments. Cameron (2025), as cited in International  
Journal of Social Science and Human Research (2025), elaborates on the façade of autonomy  
that masks systemic precarities including lack of benefits and career progression, with  
algorithmic stressors contributing to emotional fatigue, disrupted sleep patterns, and increased  
accident risk.  
3.3 Comparative Legislative and Policy Responses  
Cross-jurisdictional policy responses to the gig economy's protection gap have varied  
considerably in ambition and effectiveness. The European Union's Platform Work Directive  
(EU 2024/2831), formally adopted on 23 October 2024, represents the most comprehensive  
regulatory intervention to date. The Directive introduces a "presumption of employment  
relationship," whereby platforms are considered the employer unless they can affirmatively  
demonstrate otherwise, effectively reversing the burden of proof and extending minimum  
wage, paid holiday, and social insurance entitlements to platform workers (Premier Science,  
2025). The Directive also mandates algorithmic transparency and data protection standards.  
In the United Kingdom, the Supreme Court's 2021 ruling in Uber BV v. Aslam established that  
Uber drivers are "workers" — a middle classification between employees and independent  
contractors — entitled to minimum wage, holiday pay, and pension contributions. This judicial  
development has influenced broader legislative discussions, although a comprehensive gig  
worker protection statute remains pending.  
India has pursued a legislative approach through the Code on Social Security, 2020, which for  
the first time provided formal legal recognition to gig and platform workers by defining  
"aggregator," "gig worker," and "platform worker" and requiring aggregators to contribute  
between 1–2% of their annual turnover, capped at 5% of the amount payable to workers, into  
a dedicated Social Security Fund (Press Information Bureau, India, 2025). State-level  
legislation has further elaborated this framework: Rajasthan passed the first state gig worker  
welfare law in 2023; Karnataka enacted the Karnataka Platform-Based Gig Workers (Social  
Security and Welfare) Act in August 2025, increasing the welfare fee to 1–5% and expanding  
social security benefits beyond insurance to include health coverage, accident protection, and  
financial assistance (Bhatt and Joshi Associates, 2025); and Jharkhand, Bihar, and the draft  
Telangana Bill of April 2025 have followed suit (PRS Legislative Research, 2025).  
In the United States, the regulatory landscape remains fragmented at the federal level.  
California's Assembly Bill 5 (AB5, 2019), which applied the ABC test to classify app-based  
rideshare and delivery workers as employees, represented a significant state-level advance,  
though the industry's successful passage of Proposition 22 (2020) partially reversed it. The US  
Department of Labor's final rule of January 2024 on employee versus independent contractor  
classification introduced a multi-factor economic reality test, providing greater clarity but  
stopping short of a categorical reclassification of platform workers (US Department of Labor,  
2024).  
Brazil's President Lula introduced a bill in 2024 mandating a minimum wage for ride-hailing  
workers and capping working hours at 12 hours per day, while Japan's Freelance Act (2024)  
improves transactional fairness and timely payment protections, though it does not universally  
reclassify gig workers as employees (IJFMR, 2025).  
3.4 Organisational Responses and Emerging Best Practices  
Beyond legislative frameworks, a growing body of literature examines how platform  
organisations themselves can extend social protections to their workforces. The portable  
benefits model, most fully articulated by Sundararajan (2016) and subsequently adopted in  
modified forms by several European platforms, proposes that benefits should be attached to  
workers rather than jobs, accruing proportionally to hours worked regardless of platform. This  
model addresses the multi-platform reality of many gig workers, who simultaneously work  
across several platforms and therefore accumulate only partial benefit entitlements under any  
single employer's scheme.  
Emerald's Evidence-Based HRM literature highlights the importance of training, performance  
appraisal, and reward systems in influencing worker behaviours — mechanisms directly  
applicable to the gig context. Uddin et al. (2023) demonstrated that green training and  
development (β = 0.288, p < 0.05) and green reward systems (β = 0.267, p < 0.05) had  
significant positive effects on bank employee service behaviour, suggesting that  
institutionalised HR interventions can shape worker outcomes even in non-traditional  
employment relationships. The same logic underpins calls for platforms to develop formalised  
training programmes, transparent performance feedback systems, and benefit contribution  
mechanisms that extend welfare protections to their contracted workforces.  
Research on gig worker wellbeing further emphasises the importance of organisational design  
choices. A 2024 study of food delivery platforms in Finland found that algorithmic  
management had direct and indirect negative psychosocial influences on couriers, with hard  
algorithmic control techniques generating stress, precarity, and resistance behaviours  
(Algorithmic management, wellbeing and platform work, 2024). These findings underscore  
that social security extension is not merely a legal or financial question but a human resource  
management imperative with direct implications for worker welfare, platform sustainability,  
and service quality.  
4. Research Objectives  
The study is guided by the following research objectives:  
O1: To identify and systematically analyse the key structural mechanisms through which the  
gig economy perpetuates social security deficits for platform workers across jurisdictions.  
O2: To conduct a comparative assessment of governmental legislative and policy responses to  
the gig economy's protection gap, with particular reference to India, the European Union, the  
United Kingdom, the United States, and Brazil.  
O3: To evaluate the role of organisations and platform companies in extending employment  
protections, welfare benefits, and social security provisions to their gig workforces.  
O4: To develop an Integrated Protection Framework synthesising portable benefits  
architecture, tripartite funding mechanisms, algorithmic transparency mandates, and sector-  
specific welfare boards as a comprehensive policy prescription.  
O5: To derive managerial and policy implications for HR practitioners, platform operators, and  
government regulators engaged in the governance of the platform economy.  
5. Research Methodology  
5.1 Research Design  
This study adopts a descriptive-analytical research design, employing a systematic review  
methodology. The choice of this design is appropriate given the paper's macro-structural and  
comparative focus: the research question requires the synthesis of legislative documents, policy  
reports, and academic literature from multiple jurisdictions, rather than primary data collection  
from a single context. As Kraus et al. (2022) note, descriptive-analytical designs allow for  
systematic analysis of how institutions, structures, and socio-economic processes have been  
reconfigured by a macro-level transformation — in this case, the rise of the digital platform  
economy.  
The study is consistent with the methodological tradition established in systematic reviews of  
the gig economy literature (Alauddin et al., 2025; Pilatti et al., 2024) and follows the thematic  
synthesis framework outlined by Thomas and Harden (2008) and Yip and Zeng (2025).  
5.2 Data Sources  
The study draws on secondary data from a diverse range of institutional, governmental, and  
academic sources. Academic literature was retrieved from Scopus, Google Scholar, and JSTOR  
databases using keyword combinations including "gig economy social security," "platform  
worker protection," "portable benefits gig work," "algorithmic management precarity," and  
"gig worker welfare legislation." Only peer-reviewed articles, institutional reports, and verified  
legislative documents published between 2018 and 2026 were included.  
Institutional and governmental sources include: the European Union Platform Work Directive  
(EU 2024/2831); India's Code on Social Security, 2020 and subsequent state-level enactments  
(Rajasthan, Karnataka, Jharkhand, Bihar, and the draft Telangana Bill, 2025); the US  
Department of Labor's 2024 final rule on worker classification; the Human Rights Watch report  
"The Gig Trap" (2025); World Bank reports on social insurance for self-employed and gig  
workers (2024); NITI Aayog's "India's Booming Gig and Platform Economy" projections; PRS  
Legislative Research State Bill analyses; and the Press Information Bureau of India's official  
communications on gig worker policy.  
5.3 Analytical Framework  
The analysis is structured around four thematic pillars corresponding to the study's research  
objectives: (1) structural mechanisms of the protection gap; (2) comparative governmental  
responses; (3) organisational strategies; and (4) integrated framework development. Within  
each pillar, evidence is synthesised thematically, identifying convergent and divergent patterns  
across jurisdictions and organisational contexts. The Integrated Protection Framework (IPF)  
developed in Section 7 is derived through cross-thematic synthesis, drawing on evidence from  
all four pillars.

6. Data Analysis and Findings  
6.1 Objective 1 — Structural Mechanisms of the Protection Gap  
6.1.1 Legal Classification as the Primary Mechanism  
The analysis confirms that legal misclassification is the foundational structural mechanism of  
the gig economy's social security deficit. Across all jurisdictions examined, the majority of  
platform workers are classified as independent contractors — a status that, while legally  
coherent in a traditional self-employment context, is systematically inapposite to the  
operational realities of platform work. As identified in the literature, gig workers on platforms  
such as Uber, Zomato, or DoorDash do not independently set their prices, choose their client  
base, or define their working methods; these parameters are algorithmically determined by the  
platform. The economic substance of the relationship is that of employment, yet its legal form  
remains that of a commercial service contract.  
This classification asymmetry produces what the analysis terms a "protection inversion": the  
workers who bear the greatest economic risks — income volatility, accident liability, health  
expenses, equipment depreciation — are structurally excluded from the institutional  
mechanisms designed to socialise those exact risks. Segmented Labour Market Theory explains  
this inversion: the contractor classification is not a neutral legal designation but a structural  
mechanism for confining gig workers to the secondary labour market, maintaining platform  
profitability by externalising labour costs onto workers and the state.  
6.1.2 Algorithmic Control and Informational Asymmetry  
A second structural mechanism is the asymmetric power relationship created by algorithmic  
management. Platform algorithms control task allocation, pricing, performance rating, and  
worker deactivation — functions that, in traditional employment, would constitute managerial  
authority and trigger employment protections. Kadolkar et al. (2025) identify that algorithmic  
management has a double-edged effect on platform workers: it can enhance coordination  
efficiency and worker autonomy in some contexts, but systematically generates job insecurity,  
precarity, and psychological distress when applied through hard control techniques such as  
penalty systems, mandatory acceptance rates, and opaque deactivation triggers.  
The algorithmic opacity is particularly damaging for social security purposes. Workers who  
are deactivated without explanation lose their primary income source with no right to  
unemployment insurance, no notice period, and no access to dispute resolution mechanisms —  
because their contractor status means they have no "dismissal" and therefore no legally  
recognisable harm. This represents a fundamental capability deprivation in Sen's (1999)  
framework: the removal of the foundational resource through which workers convert digital  
access into economic participation.  
6.1.3 Regulatory Fragmentation  
A third mechanism is regulatory fragmentation — the mismatch between the cross-border  
operational scope of digital labour platforms and the geographically bounded jurisdiction of  
labour regulation. A platform company headquartered in one jurisdiction, operating in another,  
and routing transactions through a third can exploit differential regulatory environments to  
minimise social security obligations. This fragmentation is particularly acute for cross-border  
freelance platforms such as Upwork, Fiverr, and Freelancer, where jurisdictional ambiguity  
makes contribution obligations nearly impossible to enforce.

The analysis reveals three key findings from the comparative data. First, jurisdictions that have  
adopted a "presumption of employment" approach (EU) or judicial reclassification (UK)  
achieve the broadest protection coverage, because they shift the burden of proof from workers  
(who must prove employment status) to platforms (who must disprove it). This inversion is  
structurally significant: in traditional classification frameworks, workers bear prohibitive  
litigation costs to establish employment status, which platforms exploit. The presumption  
model eliminates this asymmetry.  
Second, India's welfare fund model — in which aggregators contribute a percentage of their  
turnover to a state-administered fund — represents an innovative intermediate pathway that  
avoids the politically contested reclassification debate while still generating dedicated social  
security resources. The tripartite funding model (aggregator + worker + government  
contributions) adopted in Karnataka's 2025 Act is particularly noteworthy as an equitable cost-  
sharing architecture, though the question of who ultimately bears the burden — workers  
through platform fee increases, or end-consumers through price hikes — requires further  
empirical investigation.  
Third, the US pattern of state-level experimentation without federal coordination creates a  
regulatory patchwork that large platforms navigate strategically, concentrating operations in  
low-regulation states and lobbying aggressively against protective legislation, as demonstrated  
by the Uber/Postmates challenge to AB5 and the industry campaign behind Proposition 22.  
6.3 Objective 3 — Organisational Strategies and Platform Responsibility  
Beyond legislative compliance, the analysis examines what organisations and platform  
companies can proactively do to extend protections. Three organisational-level strategies  
emerge from the evidence.  
First, voluntary portable benefits programmes represent an emerging best practice. Platforms  
that offer pro-rated benefits — where workers accrue vacation pay, accident insurance, and  
pension contributions proportional to hours worked — provide welfare coverage without  
requiring employment reclassification. This model, piloted by several European platforms  
following the UK ruling, recognises the multi-platform reality of modern gig work: because  
many gig workers split their time across several apps, any single employer scheme produces  
fractional entitlements insufficient for meaningful protection.  
Second, algorithmic transparency and appeals mechanisms are an organisational responsibility  
with direct social security implications. Workers who are deactivated without explanation lose  
both income and any contribution history they may have accumulated under welfare fund  
schemes. Karnataka's 2025 Act mandates that aggregators provide workers with a human point  
of contact for queries and establishes Internal Dispute Resolution Committees, addressing the  
frustration many workers experience when dealing with algorithm-driven management systems  
(Bhatt and Joshi Associates, 2025). Platforms that implement these mechanisms voluntarily —  
without waiting for legislative mandates — create conditions more conducive to worker  
welfare, reduced turnover, and reputational sustainability.  
Third, data sharing with government welfare portals is a critical organisational obligation.  
India's e-Shram portal — which aims to register all unorganised workers including gig workers  
— had recorded over 30.98 crore registrations as of August 2025 (PIB, 2025). However, the  
effective delivery of social security benefits under schemes such as AB-PMJAY requires that  
aggregator platforms share worker data with the government. HDFC Bank's analysis of the  
2025 Budget notes that the success of this initiative depends on seamless data sharing by  
platforms — a compliance obligation that several platforms have historically resisted on  
grounds of commercial sensitivity.  
6.4 Objective 4 — The Integrated Protection Framework  
Drawing on the four-dimensional diagnostic (Objective 1), the comparative legislative analysis  
(Objective 2), and the organisational strategies identified (Objective 3), this study proposes an  
Integrated Protection Framework (IPF) for bridging the gig economy's social security gap. The  
IPF comprises five interconnected pillars:  
Pillar 1 — Legal Architecture Reform: Governments should adopt a "presumption of  
employment" standard or introduce an intermediate "worker" status category, shifting the  
classification burden from workers to platforms. This should be accompanied by clear  
definitional criteria — building on India's SS Code's definitions of "gig worker," "platform  
worker," and "aggregator" — that provide legal certainty for both workers and platforms.  
Pillar 2 — Portable Benefits Architecture: Social security entitlements should be attached  
to workers, not jobs. A national portable benefits account — analogous to India's Universal  
Account Number (Aadhaar-linked) — should accumulate contributions from all platforms on  
which a worker operates, enabling proportional accrual of health, pension, and welfare  
entitlements regardless of multi-platform status.  
Pillar 3 — Tripartite Welfare Fund Financing: Funding for gig worker social security  
should follow a tripartite model: aggregator contributions (1–5% of turnover or payments, as  
per Karnataka's model), worker contributions (proportional to earnings), and government top-  
up contributions to ensure baseline coverage for low-earning workers. Behavioural nudge  
mechanisms — such as automated default enrolment and SMS reminders demonstrated to  
increase pension savings by 16% in Colombia (World Bank, 2024) — should be built into  
contribution collection platforms.  
Pillar 4 — Algorithmic Transparency and Governance: Platforms should be legally  
required to disclose the criteria by which algorithmic systems determine task allocation,  
performance ratings, and worker deactivation. Workers should have the right to a human appeal  
for any algorithmically generated decision affecting their income or continued platform access.  
This pillar directly addresses the power asymmetry identified in the structural mechanisms  
analysis (Objective 1) and is mandated by both the EU Platform Work Directive and  
Karnataka's 2025 Act.  
Pillar 5 — Data Integration and Monitoring: Effective delivery of social security benefits  
requires real-time integration between platform worker data and government welfare portals.  
Governments should mandate data-sharing obligations on aggregators as a condition of  
operating licences, with penalties for non-compliance. A dedicated National Welfare Board  
(analogous to India's Code on Social Security framework) should monitor scheme  
implementation, evaluate coverage gaps, and recommend adjustments on an annual basis.

7. Interpretation and Discussion  
The findings of this study confirm and extend several theoretical propositions from the  
management and labour economics literature. From a Segmented Labour Market perspective,  
the evidence is unambiguous: the gig economy has institutionalised a globally scaled secondary  
labour market in which millions of workers perform economically indispensable functions  
while being systematically excluded from the institutional protections that primary market  
workers take for granted. The platform mechanism has not created a new form of free economic  
agency; it has reproduced and digitally accelerated the structural disadvantages of traditional  
informal employment — income instability, exclusion from social insurance, power  
asymmetry — while creating the appearance of entrepreneurial freedom.  
The Capability Approach illuminates the human cost of this structural exclusion with particular  
clarity. When a delivery rider in Karnataka lacks health insurance, the consequential harm is  
not merely financial: it is a structural constraint on his capability for physical integrity,  
economic agency, and occupational safety. When India's Budget 2025 extends AB-PMJAY  
healthcare coverage to approximately 10 million gig workers (PIB, 2025), this is not a welfare  
concession but a capability expansion — a structural prerequisite for these workers to exercise  
the economic agency that the platform economy promises but consistently withholds.  
The cross-jurisdictional comparison reveals an important pattern regarding the effectiveness of  
different regulatory approaches. Reclassification-based models (EU, UK) achieve the broadest  
coverage but face the most intense platform industry resistance. Welfare fund models (India)  
achieve more rapid political consensus but risk underfunding and implementation gaps. The  
most effective emerging framework appears to be the tripartite hybrid adopted in Karnataka's  
2025 Act — which combines partial reclassification benefits (through transparency and  
grievance mandates) with welfare fund financing and algorithmic governance requirements.  
This hybrid approach merits consideration as a global policy template.  
The organisational implications are equally significant. The evidence suggests that platforms  
that proactively extend protections — rather than waiting for regulatory compulsion — create  
positive feedback loops: workers with health security and income stability demonstrate lower  
turnover, higher service quality, and greater platform loyalty, directly improving platform  
economic performance. This relationship parallels the findings of Uddin et al. (2023) in the  
green HRM literature, where institutionalised HR interventions (training, performance  
appraisal, reward systems) produced significant positive effects on employee service behaviour  
(R² = 0.56). Platform organisations that invest in their gig workforces' social protection are not  
merely fulfilling legal obligations — they are building human capital that underpins sustainable  
competitive advantage.  
8. Key Findings  
F1: The gig economy's social security deficit is not a market failure but a structural design  
outcome, produced by the deliberate application of independent contractor classification to  
workers who are functionally subordinate to algorithmic platform management. This  
classification mismatch is the primary mechanism of the protection gap.  
F2: Across all jurisdictions examined, the most effective protection regimes are those that shift  
the burden of classification proof from workers to platforms — as demonstrated by the EU  
Platform Work Directive (2024) and the UK Supreme Court's ruling in Uber v. Aslam (2021).  
F3: India's tripartite welfare fund model — as operationalised in the Code on Social Security,  
2020 and elaborated in Karnataka's Platform-Based Gig Workers Act, 2025 — represents a  
practically viable intermediate pathway that generates dedicated social security resources  
without requiring politically contested reclassification. Aggregator contributions of 1–5% of  
turnover provide a financing base that, when complemented by government top-up and worker  
contributions, can deliver meaningful health, accident, and old-age protection.  
F4: Algorithmic management constitutes an under-regulated dimension of gig worker  
vulnerability. The psychosocial risks associated with opaque algorithmic penalty systems,  
performance rating manipulation, and unexplained deactivation are not adequately addressed  
by current social security frameworks, which focus primarily on income and health protection.  
Algorithmic transparency mandates and human appeals rights are necessary complements to  
financial protection mechanisms.  
F5: The portable benefits model — attaching welfare entitlements to workers rather than  
employers and enabling proportional accrual across multiple platforms — is the architecturally  
most appropriate solution to the multi-platform reality of modern gig work. India's Aadhaar-  
linked Universal Account Number provides a technological infrastructure upon which such a  
portable benefits scheme could be constructed.  
F6: Voluntary organisational action — including pro-rated portable benefits, algorithmic  
transparency, worker data sharing with government portals, and internal dispute resolution  
mechanisms — is both ethically obligatory and commercially rational for platform companies  
seeking sustainable long-term operations in an increasingly regulated global environment.  
9. Suggestions and Policy Recommendations  
9.1 For National Governments  
Governments should enact comprehensive gig worker protection legislation that adopts a  
presumption of employment standard or creates a statutory "worker" intermediate category  
entitling gig workers to minimum wage, holiday pay, health insurance, and pension  
contributions. Where full reclassification is politically infeasible in the short term, welfare fund  
legislation on the Karnataka model — with tripartite financing, portable universal account  
numbers, and mandatory aggregator registration — provides an effective transitional  
framework. Federal coordination is essential in jurisdictions such as the United States to  
prevent regulatory arbitrage across state lines.  
9.2 For State and Regional Governments  
State governments, particularly in India, should prioritise the operationalisation of social  
security fund collection and disbursement mechanisms, integrate gig worker registration with  
existing e-Shram and Aadhaar-linked databases, and mandate aggregator data-sharing as an  
operating licence condition. The Draft Telangana Gig and Platform Workers Bill, 2025,  
represents an opportunity to incorporate the governance improvements demonstrated in  
Karnataka's Act, including Internal Dispute Resolution Committees and human points of  
contact.  
9.3 For Platform Organisations  
Platform companies should proactively implement portable benefits programmes, publish  
algorithmic transparency reports, establish worker appeals mechanisms, and share worker data  
with government welfare portals. Beyond regulatory compliance, platforms should invest in  
digital literacy and skills development programmes for their workforces, recognising — as the  
green HRM literature demonstrates (Uddin et al., 2023) — that human capital investment  
through training and development produces measurable improvements in service quality and  
worker commitment. Platforms operating in multiple jurisdictions should adopt the highest  
applicable protection standard globally, rather than the minimum required in each local market.  
9.4 For International Organisations  
The International Labour Organization should develop a comprehensive International Labour  
Standard on platform work, providing a definitional and normative framework upon which  
national legislation can converge. Cross-border portability of social security entitlements —  
analogous to bilateral social security agreements in traditional employment — should be a  
priority for platform workers engaged in cross-border freelance work, where regulatory  
jurisdiction is currently indeterminate.  
10. Conclusion  
This paper has argued that the gig economy's social security deficit is not an unfortunate side  
effect of technological disruption but a structurally engineered outcome, produced by the  
deliberate deployment of independent contractor classification to externalise labour costs and  
regulatory obligations onto workers who are functionally subordinate to algorithmic platform  
management. Through a systematic analytical review of legislative developments across seven  
jurisdictions and a theoretically grounded diagnosis of four structural protection gap  
mechanisms, the study has demonstrated that effective solutions require institutional  
innovation at both the governmental and organisational levels.  
The Integrated Protection Framework proposed in this study — synthesising legal architecture  
reform, portable benefits design, tripartite welfare fund financing, algorithmic transparency  
mandates, and data integration — provides a comprehensive, evidence-based roadmap for  
policymakers, HR practitioners, and platform operators engaged in building a more equitable  
platform economy. India's rapidly evolving legislative landscape — from the Code on Social  
Security, 2020 to Karnataka's pioneering 2025 Act to the Draft Telangana Bill — demonstrates  
that the political and institutional will to bridge the protection gap is growing. The challenge  
ahead is operationalisation: ensuring that the legislative frameworks translate into reliable,  
portable, and substantively meaningful social security entitlements for the estimated 24 million  
gig workers that NITI Aayog projects India's platform economy will employ by 2029–30.  
For the global gig economy, the EU's presumption of employment model and India's welfare  
fund model represent the two most promising poles of a regulatory spectrum. The most  
sustainable long-term solution is likely a hybrid that borrows from both: the normative clarity  
of employment presumption, operationalised through the practical architecture of portable,  
aggregator-funded welfare accounts. The achievement of this outcome requires sustained  
collaboration between governments, platforms, workers, and civil society — the same tripartite  
commitment that underpins effective social security systems in the traditional economy,  
adapted for the algorithmic age.
